# Evaluation — Simulated cohort

> **Simulated cohort.** Every number here comes from a synthetic population and a simulated 12-week period (run `sim-seed1234`, T0 = 2026-11-01). None of it is real-world evidence. Generated by `make evaluate`; do not edit by hand.

## Headline

- Primary endpoint, **completed screenings/WCVs per 100 contacts**: baseline **19.80**, Wiqaya **44.59** (difference +24.79).
- Both policies had the same capacity (4,735 slots over 12 weeks), the same invitation budget per slot and the same random draws per person (common random numbers).
- **Baseline:** today's practice. Age/sex-rule eligibility, most overdue first, one generic SMS in Arabic or English, no bundling.
- **Wiqaya:** the full pipeline. Rule-based gaps plus follow-up and cascade, ranking with the fairness floor, and a simulated reviewer who accepts 90% of flags. Contact uses consented channels in the preferred language, household bundling, the mobile van, up to 3 touches and a CHW visit after 2 non-responses.

- **Trade-off to note:** Wiqaya contacts fewer distinct people (reach 19.25% vs 41.5%). It spends repeat touches, CHW visits and bundled messages on the people it prioritises, instead of one SMS to everyone. Fewer people are touched, but more visits are completed (2,401 vs 1,219) and more gaps are closed. A pilot should decide this balance explicitly; `invites_per_slot` and `max_touches` in config/simulation.yaml control it.

## Sensitivity analysis: does the simulator flatter Wiqaya?

The response model is ours, so it could be built to favour our own design. To test this, we re-ran both policies with weaker or stronger response effects. The table shows how the Wiqaya-vs-baseline difference changes:

| Scenario | Override | Baseline /100 | Wiqaya /100 | Difference | Gaps closed % B → W | FN % B → W |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| main | — | 19.80 | 44.59 | 24.79 | 7.95 → 21.67 | 89.24 → 69.47 |
